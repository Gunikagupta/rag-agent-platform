Abstract
Connected vehicles increasingly embed AI accelerators, offering a substantial yet volatile
source of supplemental compute near the network edge. Unlike provisioned MEC hosts, ve-
hicular resources are highly dynamic: vehicles may leave the cell, become locally occupied, or
offer heterogeneous compute capacities. Therefore, exploiting vehicular resources requires
making admission decisions without knowing the compute capacity that will be available
during task execution. We present SMART, an uncertainty-aware admission mechanism
that enables an ETSI Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC) orchestrator to opportunistically
exploit vehicular compute under predictive uncertainty. SMART predicts future vehicular
capacity using a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) – whose uncertainty estimates are the
best calibrated among the evaluated forecasters at the nominal 95% level, and incorporates
its calibrated predictive uncertainty into a chance-constrained admission program reformu-
lated through Sample Average Approximation (SAA) and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)
approximations. Under a compute-only admission model, SMART admits 95.6% of tasks
while maintaining a median capacity-violation rate of about 0.81%. It achieves a favorable
admission–violation tradeoff compared with seven reactive, mean-only, and uncertainty-
aware baselines, by approaching the performance of a compute-capacity oracle under the
modeled assumptions. Finally, the sensitivity analyses show that variability in base-station
compute availability is a key determinant of admission performance, highlighting the need
for a calibrated admission and resource allocation mechanism tailored to opportunistic base-
station compute pooling.
Keywords: Multi-access Edge Computing, Vehicular Edge Computing, Bayesian Neural Net-
works, Uncertainty Quantification, Chance-Constrained Optimization, Resource Orchestration.

Introduction
European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) MEC offers a standardized execution
environment for deploying latency-sensitive services at the network edge [1]. A MEC host pro-
vides a fixed compute capacity, whereas computational demand fluctuates with vehicle density
throughout the day, leading to saturation during peak traffic and underutilization during off-
peak periods [2]. At the same time, modern vehicles increasingly embed AI accelerators rated
at 20–275 Tera Operations Per Second (TOPS), whose capacity often goes underutilized or idle
during routine driving, parking, or charging [3]. Collectively, these underutilized resources can
rival a co-located MEC server in aggregate compute capacity [4].
Recent work leverages vehicles as edge compute hosts and migrates workloads when they leave
the coverage area, positioning vehicle compute as a practical extension of MEC capacity [3,5].
However, these approaches inherit two assumptions from conventional ETSI MEC orchestration

First, the orchestrator admits tasks only after
observing the available resource pool, preventing admission from exploiting resources that are
not yet observable and forcing operators either to overprovision the static MEC tier or to rely
on costly workload migration [5]. Second, forecasts of the future resource pool are typically
reduced to point estimates, causing admission decisions to treat these predictions as exact [6,7]
without distinguishing epistemic uncertainty, arising from incomplete knowledge, from aleatoric
uncertainty, arising from inherent system variability [8]. Addressing these limitations requires
incorporating predictive uncertainty, i.e., both its magnitude and type, into admission decisions
under partial system observability.
This raises the following question: How should volatile
vehicular capacity be committed under predictive uncertainty?
This paper introduces Strategic Mobility and Resource Uncertainty Tracker (SMART), an
ETSI-compliant mechanism for uncertainty-aware admission of opportunistic vehicular compute.
Rather than proposing a new orchestration architecture, SMART augments an existing ETSI
MEC orchestrator with a probabilistic admission layer that translates predictive uncertainty
into risk-aware decisions [1]. By doing so, it enables admission decisions that explicitly account
for uncertainty in future resource availability. SMART comprises two functions. The Knowledge
Manager (KM) learns the predictive distribution of future vehicular capacity using a BNN,
separating epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty. The Decision Manager (DM) propagates this
distribution into a chance-constrained admission program, where a configurable risk level bounds
the probability of capacity violations.
Unlike prior uncertainty-aware approaches, which typically target stable cloud environments
or model uncertainty only at a coarse level [9], SMART directly incorporates calibrated predictive
uncertainty into admission decisions for highly dynamic vehicular resource pools. To preserve
deployability, it reuses existing ETSI MEC reference points and the Application Mobility Service
(AMS) for departure-driven migration, introducing no additional interfaces [1, 10]. Workload
arrivals are treated as exogenous, allowing the mechanism to isolate uncertainty in resource
availability. Overall, we make three classes of contributions:
Methodological: After a detailed literature review (cf. §2), we present SMART, a mechanism
for uncertainty-aware pooling of vehicle compute at the network edge (cf. §3), along with the
associated ETSI MEC context. The section §3 further details the two manager functions of
SMART, the BNN-based forecasting pipeline and the chance-constrained admission program.
Together, these components enable SMART to explicitly propagate predictive uncertainty into
admission decisions (cf. Fig. 1c). To the best of our knowledge, SMART is the first mecha-
nism that jointly integrates ETSI-compliant vehicular resource pooling, probabilistic resource
forecasting with separated epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty, and risk-aware admission control
under explicit uncertainty constraints.
Empirical: For empirical evaluation, we use a pseudonymized real-world taxi mobility dataset
(T-Drive [11]), containing 10 223 taxis and 2 161 Base Stations (BSs) covering 1 254 km2 of
Beijing, China.
We implement a SMART-embedded ETSI-compliant MEC mechanism and
evaluate it against a compute-capacity oracle and seven admission baselines, including reactive,
mean-based, and uncertainty-aware approaches.
Code release: The above contributions compose SMART code – an uncertainty-aware admis-
sion mechanism for opportunistic vehicular compute – a Python implementation to be released
upon acceptance of the paper. We conclude this paper in section §5.

2.0.1
Urban Vehicular Compute Pooling at the Edge
Manufacturers equip vehicles with sufficient computational capacity to satisfy peak processing
demands (cf. Fig. 1a). However, autonomous navigation, sensor fusion, and other time-critical
functions execute only intermittently, leaving substantial processing resources idle during nor-
mal operation [12] (cf. Fig. 1b). Fig. 1a illustrates the CAPEX required to provision fixed
infrastructure per BS to sustain peak vehicular demand, with C672 incurring the highest cost
and other BSs exhibiting lower investment. Fig. 1b further shows that peak demand is transient
and sustained only for a small fraction of the time across most BSs, confirming a persistent
mismatch between provisioned capacity and typical demand.
When aggregated across vehicles within a single BS coverage area, idle capacity can match
or exceed that of a conventional MEC server [12]. This aggregated capacity does not constitute
a stable infrastructure but instead forms a transient resource pool driven by daily mobility
patterns (cf. Fig. 1b). Vehicle density varies across diurnal and weekly cycles, with pronounced
morning peaks and increased weekday activity [11]. Spatially, vehicles concentrate in commercial
districts during working hours, in residential areas in the evening, and at event venues during
large gatherings. These patterns induce continuous variation in the available compute envelope
within each BS [12]. Such temporal and spatial volatility complicates orchestration: decisions
based on instantaneous observations risk acting on outdated system states, while single-point
forecasts lack robustness under distributional shifts.

2.0.2
Literature Limitations
Table 1 positions SMART against related work. Existing approaches address only subsets of
the design space. None jointly integrates ETSI-compliant vehicular resource usage (cf. Vehi-
cle usage and ETSI compliance), probabilistic forecasting with epistemic–aleatoric uncertainty
decomposition (cf. Resource prediction and Uncert. (e/a)), and uncertainty-aware admission
control (cf. Risk-aware).
Among the most closely related efforts, Feraudo et al. [5] extend ETSI MEC by incorporating
vehicular nodes, including parked far-edge hosts, into the resource pool through participation
incentives and transparent migration. Their orchestrator operates on the currently observed
pool using a reactive policy, without forecasting future capacity or modeling mobility-induced
uncertainty in admission decisions.
The remaining approaches address only parts of this design space.
Giannone et al. [13]
assume fixed and known resource capacities; Fan et al. [4] optimize vehicular offloading un-
der known resources without ETSI compliance; and AQUATOPE [9] introduces uncertainty
awareness but targets stable cloud environments without separating epistemic and aleatoric un-
certainty. Likewise, Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)-based offloading frameworks generally
assume that future resource states are known at decision time, relying on point estimates of
system dynamics [6]. Mobility-aware schedulers behave analogously, forecasting individual tra-
jectories or channel values with no uncertainty representation [7]. Both classes of approach treat
predictions as exact, failing to separate epistemic uncertainty (from incomplete knowledge) from
aleatoric uncertainty (from inherent system variability) [8].
Taken together, these limitations reveal a fundamental missing capability: uncertainty-aware
admission control over volatile vehicular resources while preserving ETSI compliance.
From
a deployment perspective, ETSI compliance is essential, as operators are unlikely to adopt
mechanisms that require non-standard MEC reference points.
Positioning: SMART addresses the literature gaps by providing the uncertainty-aware admis-
sion logic missing from existing pool-based designs while remaining fully compliance with the
ETSI MEC architecture.
The SMART admission mechanism also differs from prior uncertainty-aware decision-making
approaches. Chance-constrained and robust formulations (e.g., Gaussian-process MPC, distribu-
tionally robust optimization, and conformal prediction) typically target stable cloud workloads,
worst-case ambiguity sets, or distribution-free guarantees rather than highly dynamic vehic-
ular resource pools. In contrast, SMART integrates Bayesian capacity forecasting, epistemic–
aleatoric uncertainty decomposition, and chance-constrained admission within an ETSI-compatible
MEC orchestration path.

3
The SMART Admission Mechanism
SMART extends the ETSI MEC orchestrator with uncertainty-aware admission mechanism for
dynamic vehicular computing resources. Specifically, it addresses two challenges in opportunistic
vehicular computing: (i) admission decisions should account for future resource availability
rather than only the current state; and (ii) predicted resource availability should be treated as
uncertain rather than as deterministic point estimates because of mobility-driven fluctuations.
• Knowledge Manager (KM): uses a BNN to learn a calibrated predictive distribution of
per-cell schedulable capacity, separating epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty (§3.2);
• Decision Manager (DM): transforms predicted distributions into risk-aware admission de-
cisions by solving a chance-constrained optimization problem using SAA or CVaR approaches
(§3.3).
SMART does not introduce any additional ETSI reference points [14]. The KM module
utilizes resource-monitoring information already available within the MEC management frame-
work, while the DM module converts admission decisions into standard operations for the MEC
application lifecycle. Thus, SMART enhances orchestration while preserving interoperability
with existing ETSI MEC deployments.
Next, we present the formalization of the vehicle compute pooling problem, followed by the
KM and the BNN used to estimate predictive capacity distributions. Finally, we present the
DM and the corresponding risk-aware admission policies based on SAA and CVaR optimization.
3.1
Problem Formalization
We consider a ETSI MEC system composed of a set of cells K = {1, . . . , K}, where each cell
corresponds to the coverage area of a BS and is managed by a local orchestrator running SMART.
Time is discretized into one-second epochs. Let Vk(t) denote the set of vehicles present in cell
k at time t and Nk(t) = |Vk(t)| the corresponding vehicle count.
We assume that each vehicle i allocates a portion of its computing resources to support the
execution of external tasks. The available capacity depends on the embedded hardware charac-
teristics, which we denote by the nominal budget ci in TOPS. A fraction, ui(t) ∈[0, 1], of this
capacity is consumed onboard for applications such as perception, navigation, and infotainment.
The residual capacity available for external tasks is therefore given by ci (1−ui(t)). In a practical
deployment, vehicles periodically report their currently available compute capacity to the local
ETSI MEC orchestrator.

with a fixed compute budget, denoted c0. In this case, the orchestrator aggregates the reported
capacities of all vehicles currently present in the cell and combines them with the capacity of
the static ETSI MEC host to obtain the total schedulable capacity available in cell k at time t,

3.3.3
Problem resolution
The two formulations address different aspects of predictive uncertainty. While the SAA budget
in (12) depends on a single lower-tail order statistic, the CVaR budget in (16) averages the entire
lower-capacity tail. The two budgets diverge when the predictive distribution is highly dispersed
or asymmetric. This is particularly the case when epistemic uncertainty contributes significantly
to the total uncertainty measured by (10).
Once the risk-adjusted capacity budget is determined, both (13) and (17) admission formu-
lations can be expressed as 0/1 Knapsack Problem over the set of candidate tasks in |JW (t)|.
The capacity budget is computed from the S predictive samples, and the resulting knapsack
problem can be solved using any standard knapsack solver; in our implementation, we use a
branch-and-bound mixed-integer solver (CBC).
This work does not propose an online mechanism that adapts the risk-aware admission
parameters, ε and α, during operation. Instead, their operating regimes are characterized em-
pirically. For each cell k, ε and α are swept over a predefined grid to solve (13) and (14) on
the test windows. The resulting admission rate and capacity overshoot are then measured to
evaluate the impact of the risk parameters.
Insight: The risk-aware admission parameters ε and α govern the trade-off between task ad-
mission and the risk of capacity over-commitment. They therefore require empirical calibration
(cf. §4) to identify operating points that balance resource utilization and over-commitment risk.

We transform the raw trajectories into per-base-station vehicle counts.
We start by re-
stricting the GPS records only to the Beijing bounding box (latitude [39.76, 40.09], longitude
[116.17, 116.57]). Thereafter, we map each GPS sample to its nearest BS using a Voronoi par-
tition of the BS locations.
Finally, since the original traces are sparsely sampled, we apply
a zero-order hold (ZOH) interpolation to reconstruct a one-second vehicle association process.
Under this assumption, a vehicle remains attached to its last observed BS until it receives a new
GPS update.
At each second, the reconstructed associations define the set of vehicles present in cell k,
denoted by Vk(t), and the corresponding vehicle count Nk(t) = |Vk(t)|. These quantities are
then used to compute the schedulable capacity Ck(t) introduced in Section 3.1. In addition, each
vehicle contributes to a computing capacity of 275 TOPS, comparable to a modern automotive
accelerator. To isolate the impact of vehicular resource dynamics, we assume the MEC host
contributes no compute resources (c0 = 0), such that the total available capacity is determined
solely by the vehicles.
Finally, in order to evaluate SMART under different mobility conditions, we select 45 BSs
that exhibit the greatest temporal variability in vehicle availability. Variability is quantified
using the standard deviation of the vehicle count process Nk(t). The selected BSs are sorted ac-
cording to this metric and partitioned into three groups of 15 BSs: Stable, Moderate, and Volatile.
The corresponding thresholds are defined by the 33rd and 67th percentiles of the vehicle-count
standard deviation (4.01 and 10.66 vehicles, respectively) and remain fixed throughout the eval-
uation: 2.68 to 3.81 vehicles in Stable (median 3.40), from 4.11 to 4.91 in Moderate (median
4.57), and from 22.16 to 156.48 in Volatile (median 44.63).

4.1.2
Experimental Configuration
We train a separate KM for each BS using its own capacity history. The capacity traces are
split chronologically: the first four days for training, the fifth for validation, and the sixth for
testing. The validation day is used for temperature calibration and early stopping, while all
reported results are obtained from the held-out test day. At each decision epoch, the KM draws
S = 100 posterior samples of Ck(t + h); §4.2 shows that admission performance is insensitive
to this sample count.
Both the decision window and the prediction horizon are set to 1 s
(W = h = 1 s).
The DM is evaluated under workloads that keep each BS close to saturation. Tasks arrive at
a constant rate of 100 tasks/s throughout the test day. The compute demands are scaled so that
the average offered load matches the average schedulable capacity of each BS. This normalizes
the offered-load-to-capacity ratio across BSs while preserving differences in capacity dynamics.
Each task represents a generic Vehicular Edge Computing (VEC) inference job characterized by
its input size, compute demand wj, and deadline.
At each decision epoch, the DM solves one admission problem.
As shown in §3.3, both
the SAA (13) and CVaR (17) formulations reduce to a 0/1 knapsack problem, and are solved
using the CBC branch-and-bound solver. We also considered an oracle policy that uses the
true future capacity Ck(t+h), thereby providing the maximum achievable admission rate under
perfect knowledge. The SAA policy uses the ε-quantile of the predictive scenarios, whereas the
CVaR policy uses the average of the worst α fraction of capacity realizations.
In this sense, the parameters ε and α are independently swept over the set {0.1, 0.2, . . . , 0.9, 0.99}
to characterize the impact of risk sensitivity. Across the 45 BSs, this produces 45×(10+10+1) =
945 experimental runs. For each run, we measure the admission rate, admission loss with re-
spect to the oracle, capacity overshoot, cloud offloading, and realized capacity utilization. The
parameter sweep allows us to identify operating points that balance admission performance and
overcommitment risk and to relate them to the uncertainty characteristics of each BS (§4.3).
4.2
KM: Predictive Performance
We evaluate the KM along three complementary dimensions.
First, accuracy measures how
closely the predicted capacity matches the realized capacity. Second, uncertainty calibration to
evaluate whether predictive intervals achieve the true capacity as often as they claim. Finally,
the sensitivity to the number of posterior samples used during inference.
We compare the proposed BNN with three forecasting baselines: a deterministic Long Short-
Term Memory (LSTM) (i.e., producing a single value with no uncertainty), Monte Carlo (MC)
Dropout (i.e., approximating Bayesian inference by sampling dropout masks) [15], and a deep
ensemble (i.e., reading uncertainty from the spread of independently trained networks) [16]. All
models predict the one-second-ahead schedulable capacity Ck(t + h) on the held-out test day.

4.2.1
Accuracy
Fig. 2(a)-(b) report point accuracy. The deep ensemble achieves the lowest median Mean Ab-
solute Error (MAE) (20 TOPS), followed by the deterministic LSTM (21 TOPS), whereas MC
Dropout and the proposed BNN obtain comparable errors (35 and 36 TOPS, respectively). Point
accuracy alone, therefore, does not distinguish the BNN; its advantage lies in predictive calibra-
tion, which is discussed next. Forecasting errors are concentrated in the Volatile BS group, where
median MAE increases from tens to hundreds of TOPS, confirming that the proposed grouping
captures forecasting difficulty. The LSTM is particularly sensitive to abrupt changes, with the
worst case (BS C672) reaching 19 725 TOPS because it cannot express predictive uncertainty.
Figure 2b reports the corresponding relative error. Although Volatile BSs exhibit much larger
absolute errors, they also provide substantially larger compute capacity, keeping the BNN’s mean
absolute percentage error nearly constant across all groups (1.1%, 1.2%, and 1.0% for Stable,
Moderate, and Volatile, respectively). Since admission decisions depend on relative rather than
absolute capacity error, this explains why the Volatile group ultimately achieves admission per-
formance closest to the oracle (§4.3). As a conclusion, point accuracy alone does not distinguish
the probabilistic models; however, the consistently low relative error across regimes ensures that
forecasting errors have limited impact on admission decisions, which depend on capacity ratios
rather than absolute values.
4.2.2
Uncertainty Calibration
While accuracy measures how close the predicted capacity is to the true capacity, the calibration
asks a different question: do predictive intervals contain the real capacity as often as they claim?
Reliability. Figure 2c reports empirical versus nominal (real) coverage, where perfect calibra-
tion lies on the diagonal. The proposed BNN closely follows this perfect case across the full
range, achieving 95.7% empirical coverage at the nominal 95% level (IQR 95.3–96.3%). In con-
trast, MC Dropout (99.0%) and the deep ensemble (98.1%) consistently over-cover, indicating
overly conservative predictive intervals.
This behavior is reflected in the calibration error at the 95% level, defined as the absolute
difference between empirical and nominal coverage. The BNN achieves the lowest median error
(0.79 percentage points), compared with 3.11 for the ensemble and 4.03 for MC Dropout. The
BNN provides the best balance between predictive sharpness and calibration.
Tail calibration. Nominal 95% coverage alone is insufficient because SMART bases admission
decisions on lower predictive quantiles (12). Fig. 2d reports the coverage gap (empirical minus
nominal) across the full quantile range. A gap near zero indicates that the realized admission
risk matches the target risk. Stable and Volatile BSs exhibit opposite behaviors. Stable BSs
are over-confident, with the largest negative gap (−0.17 at ε = 0.40), whereas Volatile BSs are
under-confident, with the largest positive gap (+0.24 at ε = 0.25). These results indicates that
a single calibration temperature cannot calibrate all volatility regimes simultaneously.

4.2.3
Sample-count sensitivity
Finally, we evaluate the sensitivity of the KM to the number of Monte Carlo samples. We vary
the sample size S in (5) over S ∈{25, . . . , 1000} across all 45 BSs. Point accuracy is insensitive
to S, while calibration improves with larger sample sizes. In particular, on the Volatile group,
the spread of the 95% predictive coverage (Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP)95)
across BSs and MC trials decreases from 14.3 pp at (S = 25) to 1.9 pp at (S = 1000). However,
admission decisions stabilize at much smaller sample sizes. At S = 100, re-running the SAA
and CVaR sweeps changes the median deviation from the oracle by at most 0.02 percentage
points compared to S = 1000. As a conclusion, stable admission decisions can be obtained with
only S = 100 posterior samples, providing a favorable trade-off between decision robustness and
inference latency.
Findings: The BNN combines competitive point accuracy with substantially better calibration
than alternative probabilistic predictors, while requiring only a modest number of posterior sam-
ples for stable inference. These properties make it well suited to drive SMART’s uncertainty-
aware admission policy, whose performance depends on reliable predictive quantiles rather than
point forecasts alone.
4.3
DM: Admission Performance
Unless otherwise stated, all reported quantities are expressed as deviations from the oracle
admission. A value of zero, therefore, indicates that the admission policy matches the oracle,
which has perfect knowledge of the future capacity. The risk parameters ε (SAA) and α (CVaR)
control the conservativeness of the admission decision: increasing either parameter relaxes the
admission constraint and allows more tasks to be admitted.
4.3.1
Risk–Admission Tradeoff
Figure 3 reports the admission deviation only with respect to the oracle for the three volatility
groups. As we can observe, the behavior of SAA is more aggressive. Small values of ε lead to
conservative admission, while larger values eventually cause over-admission. At ε = 0.99, the
Stable and Moderate groups exceed the oracle admission, indicating that optimistic capacity
estimates can accept more load than the realized capacity can support. On the other hand, the
Volatile group stays nearest the oracle across the whole ε range, since, against its large capacity,
the same prediction error affects a smaller fraction of its admission.
Under CVaR, all groups under-admit at small values of α and gradually approach the oracle
as the risk level increases. The Volatile group remains closest to the oracle over the entire range
and slightly exceeds it at large α values. Stable and Moderate cells approach the oracle more
conservatively.
Finally, we observe that, for both approaches, the optimal risk parameter configuration (ε⋆
for SAA and α⋆for CVaR) depends on the volatility regime of each cell. We can see that the
Volatile group favors lower risk parameters and a more conservative approach. On the other

4.3.2
Impact of Uncertainty Decomposition
Figure 4 evaluates the contribution of epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty to the admission
decision. We considered three variants. The first variant, noted TOTAL, uses the full pre-
dictive draws. The seconde variant, noted EPIonly, keeps the posterior-mean samples µ(s)(xt)
alone. Finally, the last variant, noted ALEonly, uses the posterior mean plus fresh draws from
N(0, ˆσale). The results show that the two uncertainty components do not contribute equally to
the admission decision. In Stable and Moderate cells, the aleatoric component dominates the
lower predictive tail and largely determines the effective capacity budget. In contrast, the epis-
temic component becomes increasingly important in the Volatile group. In conclusion, removing
either component changes admission behavior, and the direction of that change depends on the
regime rather than simply on shrinking or expanding a uniform safety margin.
Findings. Epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty influence admission decisions differently across
mobility regimes. While aleatoric uncertainty primarily determines the effective admission bud-
get, the contribution of epistemic uncertainty increases in highly volatile cells, making their
explicit separation beneficial for risk-aware admission.

Comparison Baselines
Beyond the oracle comparison, we evaluate SMART against seven baselines spanning three
classes, all evaluated on the same 45 BSs and workload (Table 4). These baselines allow us to
isolate the benefits of forecasting (Class A vs. Class B), of distributional information (Class B
vs. Class C), and of the proposed probabilistic forecasters (B1 vs. B2):
- Class A - Reactive policies that do not forecast capacity: A1 admits a task if its demand fits
within the last observed capacity (adapted from [5]), while A2a and A2b greedily prioritize
tasks by decreasing (First-Fit Decreasing) and increasing demand, respectively.
- Class B - Policies using point forecasts of mean future capacity: B1 relies on the predictive
mean of the proposed BNN, whereas B2 uses a deterministic LSTM.
- Class C - Uncertainty-aware policies: C1 applies split-conformal prediction using the per-BS
residual quantile qε, while C2 adopts robust optimization with a box uncertainty set (µ−Γσ).
Table 4 compares Admission and Capacity-Violation rates across the three BS volatility groups
and baselines. A Capacity-Violation occurs when the admitted load exceeds the real capacity
during a one-second decision window.
For SMART, each BS operates at its calibrated risk level ε⋆for SAA or α⋆for CVaR), selected
such that its admission rate does not exceed the oracle. At these operating points, SMART-SAA
and SMART-CVaR admit 95.1% and 95.0% of tasks, respectively (cf. All column), effectively
matching the oracle (95.1%). In contrast, the remaining baselines admit only 75% to 77% of
tasks, except the greedy policies A2a (3.7%) and A2b (26.3%).
Across all policies, Volatile BSs achieve the highest admission rates, including under the
oracle (97.9% versus 93.5% for Stable BSs), indicating that admission performance is primarily
driven by available capacity dynamics rather than forecasting difficulty. Neither reactive policies
nor methods that rely solely on point forecasts or generic uncertainty estimates match SMART’s
performance, indicating that the gain comes from translating predictive uncertainty into a risk-
aware admission budget.
The SMART’s higher admission rate comes at the cost of more frequent Capacity Viola-
tions. Overall (cf. All column), SMART-SAA and SMART-CVaR violate capacity in 6.1% and
4.2% of admitted decisions, respectively, whereas all baselines remain below 0.5% by admitting
substantially fewer tasks. CVaR consistently provides the safer operating point, particularly for
Volatile BSs (i.e., 2.5% of violated decisions versus 8.2% for SAA), because averaging the lower
α-tail produces a more conservative capacity estimate than the single SAA quantile.

Conclusions and Future Work
We presented SMART, an ETSI-compatible mechanism for uncertainty-aware admission of op-
portunistic vehicular compute. SMART is composed of two modules: a Knowledge Manager
that learns a calibrated predictive distribution of future per-BS compute capacity using a BNN,
and a Decision Manager that performs risk-aware admission via chance-constrained optimization
using SAA and CVaR approximations.
Using a real-world taxi mobility dataset, we showed that the proposed BNN provides the
best-calibrated forecasts at the nominal 95% coverage level. Under the compute-only admission
model, SMART admits approximately 95% of tasks (i.e., 95.1% with SAA and 95.0% with
CVaR), matching the Oracle and outperforming seven reactive, point-forecast, and uncertainty-
aware baselines by approximately 23% in relative admission rate
This improvement is achieved at capacity-violation rates of 6.1% (SAA) and 4.2% (CVaR).
After accounting for violations, the fraction of tasks successfully served remains between 89%
and 91%, compared with about 77% for the best baseline. All decisions are executed within the
one-second control interval with lightweight per-BS control-plane overhead.
We analyze the operating conditions under which SMART remains effective.
Sensitivity
to variability in base-station compute availability is significant. Consequently, under abrupt
capacity drops, aggressive admission reduces available headroom, suggesting the need for com-
plementary mechanisms such as reserved capacity, proactive migration, or fallback to static MEC
resources. Overall, the homogeneous-capacity assumption used in this work isolates mobility-
driven uncertainty and should be interpreted as an evaluation setting rather than a deployment
guarantee. Future work will focus on online risk adaptation, multi-step forecasting, and extend-
ing the model to include communication and queueing effects beyond compute-only resources.
